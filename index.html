Planetary Ecology II:
The Water & Wattage of #genAI in Context

A Comparative Analysis with Data Estimates — the 2026 Update
David (Jhave) Johnston  ·  June 2026
research compiled and verified with Claude (Anthropic)
a sequel to Planetary Ecology: Carbon Emissions of #genAI in Context (May 2025)
Every prompt is a sip of electricity from a river of generation; every river of generation is fed by watersheds of fuel, sun, wind, and uranium; every watershed is shared. The question was never whether AI touches the planet — everything touches the planet. The question is: how much, compared to what, and bending toward where?

0. Preamble: Between Refusal and Rapture

A year ago, the first Planetary Ecology essay argued from the available evidence that an average person's use of generative AI was — and remains — a minor tributary of their carbon footprint: far below children, cars, crypto, combat, diet, pets, or aviation. Since May 2025, the evidence base has changed substantially, mostly for the better. For the first time, model providers published measured per-prompt figures with methodologies attached.[26][28] Peer-reviewed analyses of AI's aggregate footprint appeared in Patterns, Communications of the ACM, and Nature Computational Science.[2][3][6] The International Energy Agency built a dedicated model of data-centre demand and then, crucially, reported a year of actuals against it.[13][15]

The discourse, meanwhile, polarized. One pole treats every chatbot reply as an act of ecological violence; the other treats any environmental question as Luddite noise on the road to singularity. Both poles are innumerate. This essay occupies the position the data supports: individual use of generative AI is environmentally trivial; the aggregate industrial buildout of AI is environmentally consequential; and these two truths do not cancel each other out. Holding both at once is not fence-sitting. It is what the numbers say.

Key findings (mid-2026)

  • Per prompt: a median chatbot text query consumes roughly 0.24–0.34 Wh of electricity and emits ~0.03 g CO₂e by the providers' own measured accounting[26][30]; independent benchmarks put typical frontier-model queries at ~0.4–3 Wh, and long reasoning-model responses at 10–65× more (up to ~29–40 Wh).[5][23]
  • Per year, per user: a daily chatbot habit lands between ~0.1 and ~10 kg CO₂e/year; even an extreme reasoning-heavy or image-heavy practice (~200–400 kg/yr) remains below one round-trip transatlantic flight (~1,600 kg) and far below a car (~4,600 kg).[8][17]
  • Water: per-prompt estimates span ~0.13 mL to ~45 mL depending entirely on accounting boundary; a year of heavy chatbot use consumes less water than producing a single hamburger.[2][28][31] But two-thirds of new US data centres are sited in water-stressed regions — aggregate comfort does not erase local conflict.[35]
  • Aggregate: data centres used ~415 TWh globally in 2024 (~1.5% of electricity) and ~182 Mt CO₂ (~0.5% of energy-sector emissions); demand grew ~17% in 2025 and the IEA projects ~945 TWh by 2030.[13][15] AI-specific emissions in 2025 were an estimated 33–80 Mt CO₂e — somewhere between Denmark and Austria, and roughly 1–2% of what global militaries emit.[3][22]
  • The paradox: per-prompt efficiency improved ~33× in a single year while Google's and Microsoft's absolute emissions rose 51% and 23% over their baselines. Efficiency is winning the battle and losing the war — the textbook signature of Jevons' paradox.[26][27][29]

1. Method, Boundaries, and Honest Uncertainty

Most public disagreement about AI's footprint is not disagreement about facts; it is disagreement about boundaries, usually undeclared. Before any number in this essay, five questions:

The five boundary questions.
(1) Marginal or amortized? Does the figure cover only inference, or also a share of training and idle capacity?
(2) On-site or full-chain? Water "used by AI" can mean evaporated at the data centre (small), or also the water embedded in generating its electricity (≈10× larger), or also chip manufacturing (larger still).[2][31]
(3) Withdrawal or consumption? Water withdrawn and returned to a river is not water consumed (evaporated). Conflating them inflates estimates by an order of magnitude.[2][39]
(4) Median or mean? Google reports the median prompt; long-context and reasoning queries form a heavy tail that the median hides.[5][26]
(5) Market-based or location-based emissions? Corporate "per-prompt CO₂e" figures typically credit purchased clean energy; the local grid may tell a dirtier story.[43]

Sources here are tagged by type in the bibliography: peer-reviewed, government/agency, corporate disclosure, independent analyst, journalism. Corporate figures are used only where methodology was published, and always alongside independent estimates. Where sources conflict, the range is shown, not the convenient end. All arithmetic performed on cited figures is shown inline so it can be checked.

2. The Year of Disclosures: What a Prompt Actually Costs

For years, the most-cited estimate of a ChatGPT query's energy (~3 Wh) was an inference stacked on an inference. In February 2025, Epoch AI re-derived it from hardware first principles and got ~0.3 Wh for a typical GPT-4o query — ten times lower.[23] Then the providers spoke. Sam Altman stated the average ChatGPT query uses ~0.34 Wh and ~0.000085 gallons (~0.32 mL) of water — a claim offered without methodology, and rightly criticized for it.[30][41] Mistral, working with the French environment agency ADEME and Carbone 4, published the first full lifecycle analysis of a frontier model.[28] And in August 2025, Google released the most detailed measurement yet: the median Gemini Apps text prompt consumes 0.24 Wh, emits 0.03 g CO₂e, and evaporates 0.26 mL of water — with a published technical methodology covering active accelerators, host CPU/DRAM, idle machines, and data-centre overhead.[26][34]

Independent benchmarking complicates the comfort. Jegham et al. measured realistic workloads across models: GPT-4o ranged from ~0.42 Wh (short prompts) to ~1.79 Wh (long prompts), and reasoning models — which generate long hidden chains of thought — consumed 10–65× more energy per query, with DeepSeek-R1 and o3-class models reaching ~29–40 Wh on long prompts.[5] The earlier peer-reviewed baseline still stands: Luccioni, Jernite & Strubell found text generation around 0.05 kWh per 1,000 inferences (~0.05 Wh each on small models) but image generation up to 2.9 kWh per 1,000 — roughly 2.9 Wh per image, "nearly a phone charge" for the largest image models.[1]

Two honest caveats cut in opposite directions. Against complacency: median figures exclude the reasoning tail, agentic workflows that issue hundreds of queries autonomously, and video generation, which is orders of magnitude costlier per output.[5][44] Against alarm: the efficiency curve is steep — Google reports the energy of its median prompt fell 33× and its carbon footprint 44× in twelve months through software, hardware, and scheduling improvements.[26] Per-unit cost is a moving target, and it is moving down fast.

3. The Individual: A Year of Use, In Context

Translate prompts into a year of living. Thirty queries a day for a year is ~11,000 queries. Under Google's measured accounting that is ~2.6 kWh and ~0.33 kg CO₂e — the emissions of driving an average car about one mile.[17][26] Under the harsher independent benchmark (~1 Wh/query, average US grid at ~0.37 kg CO₂/kWh), it is ~4 kg CO₂e/year.[5][18] A genuinely extreme practice — a hundred long reasoning queries daily, or a thousand generated images a day, every day — reaches roughly 200–400 kg CO₂e/year.[1][5] That extreme is still smaller than the annual footprint of a mid-sized dog, and a quarter of one transatlantic round trip.[8][12] The 2025 essay's central claim survives its second year of data, now with better numbers attached.

None of this is a permission slip. It is a proportionality claim. A person who interrogates your chatbot use while flying to conferences and eating beef has confused the visible novelty with the invisible baseline. Equally: a person who cites these bars to dismiss all environmental questions about AI has confused the per-user numerator with the planetary denominator — to which we now turn.

4. Water: The Argument That Changed the Most

Water became the sharpest front of the AI-environment debate, and 2025–2026 delivered the data to adjudicate it. The peer-reviewed anchor is Li, Yang, Islam & Ren's Making AI Less "Thirsty" (arXiv 2023; Communications of the ACM, 2025): training GPT-3 consumed ~700,000 L of on-site water (~5.4 million L including off-site), GPT-3-era inference "drank" a 500 mL bottle per 10–50 medium responses, and global AI demand could withdraw 4.2–6.6 billion m³ in 2027 — half a United Kingdom's annual withdrawal.[2] Google's 2025 disclosure then reported 0.26 mL per median prompt — three orders of magnitude below the popular "bottle of water per conversation" meme. Both are defensible; they measure different boundaries. Google counts on-site cooling evaporation for a median prompt on efficient infrastructure; Ren et al. count on-site plus the water evaporated generating the electricity, for an older model — and Ren publicly criticized Google's figure for omitting exactly that indirect share.[26][43] Mistral's full-lifecycle accounting (45 mL per 400-token response, upstream included) shows what the widest boundary looks like.[28]

The data-dense independent treatment is SemiAnalysis's March 2026 analysis, From Tokens to Burgers — A Water Footprint Face-Off.[31] They bottom-up modelled xAI's 400 MW Colossus 2 facility in Memphis: ~267 million gallons evaporated annually for cooling, ~66 million withdrawn-but-returned for adiabatic flushing, plus ~13 million gallons/year of amortized chip-manufacturing water — ~346 million gallons/year total, a water-use effectiveness of 0.51 L/kWh. Their comparisons are deliberately vertiginous: one hamburger (245 gallons embedded) equals the water footprint of using Grok thirty times a day for 668 years; the entire Colossus 2 campus consumes only ~2.4× the water of the burgers sold by a single In-N-Out restaurant.[31]

The aggregate picture from government data agrees on proportions. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory put direct US data-centre water consumption at ~17.4 billion gallons in 2023, projected to 38–73 billion by 2028; indirect water embedded in their electricity was ~211 billion gallons.[16][39] US golf courses irrigate ~1.5 billion gallons per day — roughly 548 billion gallons a year, about thirty times the direct consumption of every data centre in the country.[19] Agriculture operates three further orders of magnitude up.

And yet. The same months produced Bloomberg's finding that roughly two-thirds of new US data centres built or planned since 2022 sit in regions of high water stress — 26 in Arizona, 26 in Texas, 67 in Virginia — with similar or worse siting patterns in China, India, and the Gulf.[35] The Dalles, Goodyear, Querétaro, and Memphis are real places with real aquifers. The correct synthesis is not "AI barely uses water" (true globally, sometimes false locally) nor "AI is drinking the planet" (false at every scale). It is: water impact is a siting and disclosure problem, not a per-prompt problem. A prompt is a drop; a gigawatt campus in a desert is a policy choice.[39][40]

5. The Aggregate: Where the Real Numbers Live

The planetary question is not your prompt; it is the buildout. In 2024, global data centres (all computing, not just AI) consumed ~415 TWh — ~1.5% of world electricity — emitting ~182 Mt CO₂ from their power generation, ~0.5% of energy-sector emissions.[13][36] In 2025 their consumption grew ~17%, with AI-focused facilities growing fastest; the IEA's satellite tracking shows "AI factory" capacity more than tripling in eighteen months.[15] The first peer-reviewed isolation of the AI share, de Vries-Gao in Patterns, estimates AI systems alone at 32.6–79.7 Mt CO₂e and 312–765 billion litres of water in 2025 — a small European country's footprint, growing fast from a small base.[3]

The corporate trajectories tell the aggregate story most honestly. Google's per-prompt efficiency improved 33× in a year — and its total "ambition-based" emissions still rose 11% in 2024 to 11.5 Mt CO₂e, up 51% since 2019, even as it cut data-centre energy emissions 12% while growing data-centre electricity 27%.[27] Microsoft's emissions are up 23.4% since 2020, attributed by the company to AI data-centre construction.[29] Hyperscaler capital expenditure exceeded $400 billion in 2025 and is projected near $600 billion in 2026; gas-turbine orders are at a 25-year high, with 15–27 GW of on-site gas projected for US data centres by 2030 and turbine lead-times stretching years.[14][15] Whatever the per-prompt bar charts show, the industry is pouring concrete, signing nuclear-restart contracts, and ordering gas turbines as if the curve goes up and to the right. Watch what they build, not what they tweet.

6. Extrapolating the Curve — With the Humility the Curve Has Earned

Now the requested extrapolation, prefaced by its own failure history. In 1999, Forbes published "Dig More Coal — the PCs Are Coming," claiming the internet already used 13% of US electricity and would soon use half. The real figure was ~1–2%, and Koomey spent a decade correcting it.[32] Between 2010 and 2018, global data-centre compute grew ~550% while electricity use grew ~6% — efficiency absorbed nearly all of demand growth (Masanet et al., Science).[7] Every extrapolation in this section must be read with that correction record in mind.

That said, this cycle differs from 2000 in three measurable ways. First, the demand is no longer hypothetical: 2025's ~17% consumption growth was metered, not projected.[15] Second, the capital is committed — hundreds of billions of dollars of accelerators, shells, and substations.[14] Third, the physical bottlenecks are visible: ~20% of planned projects face grid-connection delay risk, transformer wait-times have doubled, and scarcity is now priced into electricity markets near major clusters.[14][15]

The central scenario band, mid-2026:

Electricity: global data centres ~415 TWh (2024) → ~945 TWh (2030, IEA base; scenario range roughly 700–1,250 TWh), reaching just under 3% of global electricity; US share 4.4% (2023) → 6.7–12% (2028, LBNL).[13][16]
Carbon: data-centre emissions ~182 Mt (2024) → ~1% of global CO₂ by 2030 in the IEA's central case (~1.4% in fast-growth), ~350 Mt by 2035; AI-specific emissions 33–80 Mt (2025) plausibly doubling or more by 2030.[3][13][36]
Water: US direct data-centre consumption 17.4 → 38–73 billion gallons (2028); global AI-attributable withdrawal up to 4.2–6.6 billion m³ (2027, upper-bound academic estimate).[2][16]
Matter: 1.2–5.0 Mt of cumulative AI-server e-waste by 2030 (reducible 16–86% by circular practices); embodied carbon of chips and construction remains the largest disclosure gap.[6][10]

Even the high ends keep AI well under 2% of global emissions by 2030 — a fraction of today's military (~5.5%) or agriculture, in the neighbourhood of aviation's 2.5% only in its most aggressive scenarios.[22][24] But two asymmetries should temper comfort. The first is direction: aviation and crypto are roughly flat; AI is the fastest-growing industrial electricity load on Earth, and is driving gas-plant life-extensions and new fossil capacity in specific grids now, whatever the 2030 averages say.[14][15] The second is Jevons: when Satya Nadella invoked Jevons' paradox in January 2025, he was making a business forecast — efficiency gains lower the price of intelligence, which multiplies consumption. The 33×-per-prompt / +51%-absolute pattern of 2025 is that paradox operating exactly as Jevons described coal in 1865. Efficiency alone has never capped a useful technology's total consumption; only saturation, price, or policy has.[7][26][27]

The honest extrapolation, then, is a cone, not a line: AI ends the decade somewhere between "another aviation" (a real, bounded, ~1–2% wedge demanding real decarbonization) and "another false coal alarm" (efficiency and saturation flattening the curve as they did in 2010–2018). The IEA's own counter-ledger deserves its place in the cone: AI-enabled optimization of grids, buildings, transport, and materials could plausibly avoid more emissions than data centres emit — a potential, not a promise, and one no company should be allowed to claim as an offset without showing the arithmetic.[13]

7. The Nuanced Position, Restated for 2026

To the friend who questions your daily AI use: your year of prompting costs the atmosphere less than one tank of gasoline, and the watershed less than one hamburger. The data on this is no longer hand-waving; it is measured, audited in one case by a national environment agency, and replicated by independent benchmarks.[5][26][28] Refusing a tool of genuine cognitive value on per-use environmental grounds, while driving, flying, and eating as the culture does, is not environmentalism; it is aesthetics.

To the accelerationist who waves the question away: the buildout you celebrate added more emissions at Google and Microsoft in five years than per-prompt efficiency can hide, is two-thirds sited in water-stressed counties, is extending the lives of gas and coal plants in specific grids, and is governed by companies of which only two have published audited footprints — with OpenAI and Anthropic disclosing essentially nothing standalone.[27][29][33][35] Trivial per-use and consequential in aggregate is also how automobiles looked in 1926.

The defensible demands are structural, not personal: (1) mandatory, boundary-explicit disclosure of energy, carbon (location-based as well as market-based), water, and embodied impacts per model and per facility — the Google and Mistral disclosures prove this is feasible[10][33]; (2) siting and cooling standards that treat water-stressed basins differently from wet ones[35][39]; (3) additionality in clean power — new carbon-free generation matched to new load, not certificates shuffled across a continent[36][43]; and (4) circularity requirements for the coming wave of accelerator e-waste.[6]

Coda

The first essay ended in molecular interconnection: carbon does not care whether it was exhaled by a jet engine, a server farm, a golf-course mower, or a child. A year later the accounting is better, and it says what it said before, more precisely: the prompt in your hand is almost weightless; the infrastructure rising behind it is not, yet remains — for now — a small wedge of a vast and warming whole. Neither guilt nor rapture is a measurement. Attention is. Keep measuring.
